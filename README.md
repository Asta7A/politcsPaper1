AQA A-LEVEL POLITICS — PAPER 1 (7152/1) MASTER GUIDE
Government & Politics of the UK — A* Optimised
Built from official AQA mark schemes and examiner guidance

PAPER OVERVIEW
You have 2 hours. The paper is worth 77 marks (33.3% of your A-Level).
Section A — Three 9-mark questions. All compulsory. (~10 min each = 30 min total)
Section B — One 25-mark extract question. Compulsory. (~35 min)
Section C — Two 25-mark essay questions. Answer ONE. (~35 min)
Reserve 5 minutes at the end to check your Section C conclusion does not contradict your argument.

ASSESSMENT OBJECTIVES DECODED
Everything the examiner marks maps to one of three AOs. Understand them and you know where every mark comes from.
AO1 — Knowledge & Understanding: Accurate facts, concepts, political vocabulary, named examples. Satisfy this by naming the correct concept, using precise terminology, and deploying specific dated examples — legislation with its year, election results with seat and vote-share data, named court cases, named reports.
AO2 — Analysis: Developing arguments, explaining why something matters, sustaining a focused line of reasoning rather than describing events. Every paragraph needs an explicit "this demonstrates that…" or "this is significant because…" moment.
AO3 — Evaluation: Weighing competing arguments, making mini-conclusions within paragraphs, producing a final conclusion that flows from and is supported by your preceding discussion. Never assert — always justify.
Mark distribution by question type:

9-mark: AO1 = 6 marks, AO2 = 3 marks
25-mark extract: AO1 = 5 marks, AO2 = 10 marks, AO3 = 10 marks
25-mark essay: AO1 = 5 marks, AO2 = 10 marks, AO3 = 10 marks

Critical insight: On 25-mark questions, AO2 and AO3 together = 20 marks. AO1 = only 5. A student with sharp analysis and fewer examples will outscore a student who lists facts without evaluating them. Analysis and evaluation are where A* is won.

SECTION A — 9-MARK QUESTIONS
Question format: "Explain and analyse THREE ways in which [X]…"
Mark scheme levels
Level 3 (7–9 marks): Detailed knowledge of all three points. Thorough explanations. Accurate, specific named examples for each point. Analysis is structured, clearly focused on the question, and confidently developed into a coherent answer.
Level 2 (4–6 marks): Generally sound knowledge. Some development with generally appropriate examples. Analysis developed in most places but some points remain descriptive. MAXIMUM LEVEL IF ONLY TWO RELEVANT POINTS ARE MADE.
Level 1 (1–3 marks): Limited knowledge. Limited examples. Analysis takes the form of description. MAXIMUM LEVEL IF ONLY ONE RELEVANT POINT IS MADE.
Hard rule
Two points = maximum 6 marks. One point = maximum 3 marks. You must make three distinct points in three distinct paragraphs to access 7–9. Never merge two points into one paragraph.
The formula — repeat three times
Step 1 — POINT: State your point in one sentence using precise political vocabulary. Name the mechanism or concept immediately.
Step 2 — EXPLAIN: Define or elaborate on what this concept means in political terms. Show you understand it, not just that you can name it.
Step 3 — EXAMPLE: Deploy a specific, named example. Legislation with its date. An election result with year and seat/vote data. A named court case. A specific politician or named report. Vague examples lose AO1 marks.
Step 4 — ANALYSE: Link the example back to WHY it demonstrates your point. Use your analysis starters here.
Model paragraph
"Firstly, [point using precise political vocabulary]. This refers to [definition/elaboration]. One particular example illustrating this is [named example with date/data]. This demonstrates that [link back to the question], which is significant because [impact/implication]."
Aim for approximately 120–150 words per point. Three paragraphs at this length secures Level 3.

SECTION B — 25-MARK EXTRACT QUESTION
Question format: "Analyse, evaluate and compare the arguments in the above extracts regarding [X]."
AO1 = 5, AO2 = 10, AO3 = 10. Compulsory. ~35 minutes.
The examiner's hard rule — cannot be ignored
"Students who fail to focus their discussion on the arguments in the articles, however complete their answer may otherwise be, cannot achieve above Level 2." A brilliant general essay about the topic with no direct engagement with the extracts = maximum 10 out of 25. You must engage with specific arguments made in the texts throughout your answer.
Mark scheme levels
Level 5 (21–25): Detailed and accurate knowledge supports analysis. Analysis is balanced and confidently developed. Comparisons are well-explained, focused, and fully supported with developed examples. Evaluation leads to well-substantiated conclusions. Provenance and perspective of each extract is successfully evaluated. Sustained analytical focus throughout.
Level 4 (16–20): Accurate knowledge. Analysis balanced and developed. Comparisons relevant and supported with examples. Conclusions show some substantiation. Provenance evaluated though could be further developed.
Level 3 (11–15): Generally sound knowledge with some inaccuracies. Analytical points made but some are descriptive. Comparisons made, may be supported. Conclusions consistent but lack substantiation. Provenance commented on but evaluation lacks depth.
Paragraph-by-paragraph structure
Paragraph 1 — INTRODUCTION (provenance and overview): Immediately identify the key phrases and define and reference the extract. and begin your key judgement from the begining. This is where AO3 evaluation begins earning marks from your very first sentence.
Use: "One side of the debate suggests [Extract 1's position]. In contrast, the weaker side of the debate suggests [Extract 2's position]. Overall, the strongest argument for [position] is [brief signal of your conclusion].
Paragraph 2 — ARGUMENT FROM EXTRACT 1: Identify a specific argument from Extract 1. Reference or closely paraphrase the author's claim. Explain what the author means. Extend the argument with your own political knowledge — named examples, case law, statistics, legislation. This is where AO1 depth is demonstrated.
Use: "Firstly, Extract 1 argues that [specific argument]. One particular example illustrating this is [named example with date/data]. This has been the case when [contextual detail]. This is significant because [analysis]. This means that [implication for the question]."
Paragraph 3 — COMPARISON WITH EXTRACT 2: Immediately compare with Extract 2's position on the same issue. Use direct comparative language. Extend Extract 2's argument with your own knowledge. This earns AO2 comparison marks.
Use: "In contrast, Extract 2 argues that [opposing argument]. Yet, others argue that [elaboration of Extract 2's position]. Evidence illustrating this is [named counter-example with date/data]. This demonstrates that [analysis of the counter-argument]. This has an impact because [significance]."
Paragraph 4 — MINI-EVALUATION: Weigh the two arguments. Which is more convincing and why? Consider the author's perspective and bias, the date of publication, and the quality of evidence cited.
Use: "Yet the stronger argument is [Extract 1 or 2] because [evidenced reason]. Argument A is stronger than Argument B because [comparison grounded in evidence and provenance]."
Repeat this cycle — Argument, Comparison, Mini-evaluation — for a second major theme from the extracts. Aim for two to three full cycles in a Level 5 response.
Final paragraph — CONCLUSION: Make a clear, substantiated final judgement on which extract makes the more convincing overall case. The conclusion must flow logically from your preceding analysis. Do not introduce new material.
Use: "In conclusion, [clear position on which argument is stronger]. Overall, [Extract X] makes the more convincing case because [evidenced justification referencing your paragraphs]. While [acknowledge the strongest point of the other extract], this is ultimately less convincing because [reason]."
Provenance — the AO3 marks most students miss
For every extract, address the following in your response:
Who wrote it? Academic, politician, journalist — what agenda might they have?
Where was it published? Academic journal suggests credibility. Tabloid implies populist framing. Party publication signals partisan purpose.
When was it written? Is it pre or post a major political event that affects the validity of the argument?
Why might the author hold this view? Political affiliation, professional background, ideological position.
From official AQA examiner commentary (2022 paper): Top responses noted that Extract 2 was written by a Conservative MSP, making his opposition to a codified constitution politically motivated. Examiners rewarded this explicitly as AO3 evaluation of perspective. This is the difference between Level 4 and Level 5.

SECTION C — 25-MARK ESSAY QUESTION
Question format: "'[Statement].' Analyse and evaluate this statement."
AO1 = 5, AO2 = 10, AO3 = 10. Choose ONE from two. ~35 minutes.
The synoptic link requirement — mandatory for Level 5
"Any response that does not include synoptic points cannot achieve above Level 4." Synoptic links mean explicitly connecting your answer to other areas of the specification. No synoptic links = maximum 20 out of 25, regardless of answer quality.
Mark scheme levels
Level 5 (21–25): Detailed and accurate knowledge supports analysis. Analysis is balanced and confidently developed. Synoptic links are well-explained, focused, and fully supported with developed examples. Evaluation leads to well-substantiated conclusions. Relevant perspectives successfully evaluated. Well-organised with sustained analytical focus.
Level 4 (16–20): Accurate knowledge. Analysis balanced and developed. Synoptic links relevant and supported with examples. Conclusions show some substantiation. Perspectives evaluated, though could be further developed.
Level 3 (11–15): Generally sound knowledge with inaccuracies. Analytical points made but some are descriptive. Synoptic links made but explanation lacks depth. Conclusions consistent but lack substantiation.
Paragraph-by-paragraph structure
Paragraph 1 — INTRODUCTION: Frame the debate precisely. Define the key concept(s) in the question. Acknowledge that arguments exist on both sides. Signal the direction of your overall argument — a clear thesis impresses examiners. Do not list all your points.
Use: "One side of the debate suggests [position supporting the statement]. In contrast, the weaker side of the debate suggests [counter-position]. Overall, the strongest argument for [your judgement] is [brief signal of conclusion]."
Paragraphs 2 and 3 — ARGUMENTS FOR THE STATEMENT: Two to three paragraphs confirming the statement. Each paragraph follows this internal structure exactly:
"Firstly, [point for the statement]. One particular example illustrating this is [named example with date/data]. This has been the case when [contextual detail]. One of the strongest arguments is [X] because [reason]. This is significant because [analysis]. This means that [implication for the question]. Yet, overall, [mini-conclusion weighing this argument's strength]."
Paragraphs 4 and 5 — COUNTER-ARGUMENTS: Two genuinely developed counter-arguments challenging the statement. A serious counter-argument you then outweigh is more impressive than a token one you dismiss. Same internal structure:
"On the other hand, [counter-argument]. In contrast, [elaboration of opposing position]. Another significant example is [named counter-example with date/data]. This was further exampled when [additional evidence]. This demonstrates that [analysis of counter]. This is important because [significance]. Yet the stronger argument is [weigh and conclude — return to your thesis]."
Paragraph 6 — SYNOPTIC LINK: Explicitly connect to another area of the specification. This paragraph unlocks Level 5.
Use: "It can also be seen that [synoptic connection to another part of the specification]. Evidence illustrating this is [named example from the linked area]. This has an impact because [analysis of the synoptic link and what it adds to your argument]."
Final paragraph — CONCLUSION: Take a clear, evidenced position. Justify it by weighing the relative strength of your arguments. Reference your best evidence from the body. Do not introduce new material.
Use: "In conclusion, [clear position on the statement]. Overall, [the strongest argument is X] because [evidenced justification referencing your paragraphs]. While [acknowledge the strongest counter-argument], this is ultimately less convincing because [reason]. Therefore, [final verdict on the statement]."
Balance does not mean equal weight
Balance means engaging seriously with both sides before making a clear, evidenced judgement. Three strong arguments for the statement, two serious counter-arguments, and a reasoned conclusion is perfectly balanced and will score Level 5. Do not neutralise your argument in the conclusion by sitting on the fence.
Synoptic links by topic
Parliament: elections (mandate theory), pressure groups (scrutiny), rights (HRA judicial review), devolution, PM and Cabinet.
Prime Minister and Executive: Parliament (scrutiny mechanisms), constitution (prerogative powers), elections (mandate), party dynamics.
Constitution: judiciary and Supreme Court, devolution, rights (HRA/ECHR), parliamentary sovereignty.
Democracy and Participation: electoral systems, pressure groups, referendums, parties, devolution.
Judiciary: constitution, rights (HRA), parliamentary sovereignty, devolution.

Counter-arguments:

On the other hand…
In contrast…

Mini-conclusions (use after every argument paragraph):

Yet, overall…
Yet the stronger argument is…
Argument A is stronger than Argument B because…

Main conclusion:

In conclusion…
Overall…

Strategic note: Never use the same analysis starter in consecutive sentences. Examiners notice repetition and it signals shallow analysis. Rotate: "This shows that…" then "This is significant because…" then "This implies…" then "This demonstrates that…"

PRE-SUBMISSION CHECKLIST
Run through this in the final 5 minutes.
9-mark questions:

Three distinct points in three distinct paragraphs
Each point has a specific named example with year and data where possible
Each example linked back to the question with explicit analysis
Political vocabulary used throughout

25-mark extract question:

Provenance of both extracts addressed — who, where, when, why, and implication for credibility
Specific arguments from both extracts engaged with directly, not just the general topic
Direct comparisons made between extracts — not just both discussed separately
Own political knowledge used to extend and support arguments from the extracts
Mini-evaluations present within body paragraphs using your evaluation starters
Clear, substantiated conclusion that follows from the argument

25-mark essay:

Arguments both for and against the statement are developed
At least one synoptic link to another area of the specification
Specific named examples with dates and data in every body paragraph
Mini-conclusions after each argument paragraph using your evaluation starters
Conclusion takes a clear, evidenced position — no sitting on the fence
Conclusion does not introduce new material not argued in the body

All questions:

Political vocabulary throughout — never general language where a precise term exists
No purely descriptive paragraphs — every paragraph has explicit analysis
Every question answered fully before moving to the next


THE A* PRINCIPLE
Knowledge tells the examiner what you know. Analysis tells the examiner that you understand it. Evaluation tells the examiner that you can think about it. An A* requires all three — in that order, in every single paragraph.
The examiner's first question when reading your answer is: "Is this candidate analysing, or describing?" If you can read a sentence and ask "so what?" without finding the answer in the next sentence, you have written description. Add the analysis. Every time.
